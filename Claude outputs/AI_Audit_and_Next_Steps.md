# AI-language audit — 8 research papers

Honest note first: the "rather than" count I used earlier for Rust Signature/Fringe Effect is a rough proxy, not proof. A lot of "rather than" here is just normal methods writing ("tested X rather than Y") — that's fine, real researchers write like that. What actually reads as AI is a specific *cadence*: dense abstract-style closing sentences, "first this, then that" parallel structures, and "X is neither A nor B" negation pairs. Below I've picked out the sentences that actually have that cadence, not just the keyword. Rewrite these; leave everything else alone.

Priority order (most AI-cadence sentences to least) — do these first if time is short: **GPIE, DJ, GA, SS, ECO, CL, BO, GI**.

## 1. GPIE (Green Policy Intelligence System) — highest priority
- L27: *"Difference-in-Differences remains among the most widely used quasi-experimental methods in policy evaluation, valued for isolating a treatment's effect..."* — reads like a textbook definition, not you explaining your own choice.
- L231: *"This nuanced result followed directly from a validation process that first identified, and then corrected, a flawed initial specification — one that had appeared statistically significant but was in fact spurious..."* — the "first X, then Y — one that appeared A but was in fact B" shape is a very recognizable AI pattern.
- L233: *"The framework developed here is directly transferable to future evaluations of EU environmental policy, and to other jurisdictions facing the same structural identification challenge..."* — generic "broader implications" closer. Say specifically what you'd reuse and where, in your own words.
- L229 (final conclusion sentence): dense single-sentence summary cramming the whole result — break it into 2-3 plain sentences the way you'd actually say it.

## 2. DJ (Double Jeopardy)

Say it straight: what did you actually find, in one sentence.
- L37: *"A separate, substantial body of literature documents that formal protected-area designation frequently fails to translate into effective on-the-ground management, a phenomenon widely termed the 'paper park' problem..."* — textbook lit-review voice.
- L199: *"The central finding — that ecosystem degradation isn't uniform across type — lines up directly with the divergent literature trajectories reviewed above..."* — same dense-summary issue as GPIE.

**HOTOSM — why it's been a headache and how to actually start (no more portal-wandering):**
The reason it's confusing is that most people try to start cold on the Tasking Manager itself, which dumps you into a project list with no guidance. Skip that path. Do it in this order instead:
1. Make an OpenStreetMap account at openstreetmap.org/user/new (2 minutes).
2. Don't open the Tasking Manager yet. Go to `learnosm.org` and do the "iD editor" beginner tutorial first — 20-30 minutes, and it's literally guided practice, not a live project.
3. Join a live mapathon instead of mapping solo. HOT and OSM communities run these constantly (check HOT's Slack at slack.hotosm.org, #mapper-support channel, or search "OSM mapathon" for one coming up) — someone walks you through a real task live, which is the actual fix for the "I don't get it" wall you hit.
4. Only after step 3, go to tasks.hotosm.org, filter by "Beginner" difficulty, and pick one task (usually tracing buildings or roads from satellite imagery for a disaster-response or health-access project).
5. Submit your edits — a validator reviews them, that's normal and not a judgment on you.

Total time to your first real contribution this way: under an hour, most of it in step 2-3, not fighting the interface alone.
