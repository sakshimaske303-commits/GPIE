So here's a problem that trips up a lot of policy-evaluation studies, and it's the first thing I had to solve before any statistics could even begin.

The European Green Deal applies to all twenty-seven EU countries at once. Every single one, simultaneously. Which means there's literally no EU country left over to use as an "untreated" comparison group.

And here's a detail most people evaluating EU policy miss, actually, and it's a legal detail, not a statistical one — the European Climate Law is a Regulation, specifically Regulation (EU) 2021/1119, not a Directive. That distinction genuinely matters for this project's design. A Directive would've needed each member state to transpose it into national law on its own separate timeline, which would've given me twenty-seven different, messy treatment dates. A Regulation takes direct legal effect, on one single calendar date, across every member state simultaneously. Which is exactly what makes a clean, single treatment date even possible for a DiD design in the first place. If this had been a Directive, this whole econometric approach would've needed to look completely different.

Without a genuine untreated group, you cannot statistically tell the difference between "the policy worked" and "pollution was declining anyway, for completely unrelated reasons." You need something real to compare against.

So — bring in a control group from outside the EU entirely. Three countries: the UK, which left the EU in 2020, so it's outside Green Deal jurisdiction from 2021 onward. Norway, never an EU member, but economically and geographically about as comparable as it gets. And Switzerland, same story.

Let me actually show you this directly instead of just describing it — let me pull it up in QGIS.

So this layer here is the EU-27 boundary set — Eurostat's NUTS dataset, specifically NUTS Level 0. Quick context on that naming, since it trips people up — NUTS is the EU's official statistical-region hierarchy, and Level 0 means country-level. Level 1, 2, 3 go progressively finer, down to sub-regional units used for things like regional funding allocation. I only need Level 0 here since the whole causal model operates at the country level.

And this second layer is the three control countries — but here's a detail that's easy to get wrong, and I actually got it wrong the first time. NUTS technically already includes geometries for Norway and Switzerland, because they're EFTA members inside the European Economic Area statistical framework, even though they're not EU members. It would've been tempting to just pull all three control countries straight out of NUTS too. I deliberately don't do that. I use GADM — a completely separate, non-EU boundary database — for all three control countries instead, specifically so the boundary source, the digitization standard, and the coastline vertex precision stay internally consistent across the whole control group, rather than mixing two differently-sourced boundary datasets for what's supposed to be one clean comparison group.

That decision is actually why I hit a bug early on — an EFTA double-counting bug, where a couple of these countries were getting pulled in from both NUTS and GADM simultaneously during the boundary merge, before I explicitly excluded them from the NUTS side. Caught it in a basic record-count check. Small bug, but exactly the kind of thing that silently wrecks a regression if you don't catch it before the actual statistics even start.

Both boundary sets, by the way, are kept in WGS84 — EPSG 4326 — the same coordinate reference system Sentinel-5P's data is natively delivered in. That's a deliberate choice, not a default I left alone. Keeping one single CRS through the entire pipeline, from boundaries to satellite data to the final zonal statistics, avoids reprojection error accumulating at every processing step, which matters more than people think when you're aggregating fine-resolution satellite pixels into country-level averages.

And the statistical logic underneath all of this is called difference-in-differences. Instead of comparing raw pollution levels, I'm comparing the change over time in the EU group against the change over time in this control group I just showed you. Any general Europe-wide trend that affects both groups equally just cancels out mathematically. What's left over is, in theory, the part of the change actually attributable to the EU's climate law specifically.

This two-group logic isn't something I invented, by the way — it's structurally the same idea behind one of the most famous studies in applied economics, Card and Krueger's 1994 minimum-wage study, comparing New Jersey against Pennsylvania after New Jersey raised its minimum wage. Completely different topic, same underlying trick — find a comparable neighbor that didn't get the policy, and let the comparison do the work.

Treatment date is the 30th of June 2021 — the exact day the European Climate Law legally entered into force.

Before I show you the actual pollution numbers, one minute on something most people never stop to ask about a dashboard like this — how does a satellite eight hundred kilometers up actually "see" a gas?

It's not a photo, it's spectroscopy. NO2 absorbs sunlight at a very specific wavelength band, and TROPOMI's spectrometer measures how much of that specific light got absorbed on the way down and back up. The physics is the Beer-Lambert law — I equals I-naught, times e to the power of negative sigma N L. Invert that, and you get what's called a slant column density, which then gets corrected for viewing geometry into a vertical column density — that's the actual number GPIE uses everywhere.

Here's a fact I think is a genuinely elegant reason NO2 specifically works so well for this — its atmospheric lifetime is only a few hours, sometimes up to about a day. Compare that to CO2, which lingers for centuries. That short lifetime means the column TROPOMI measures on a given day is telling you about emissions from basically that same day — not some blurred accumulation from decades of history. Exactly the property you want in a variable that's supposed to respond to a law that changed in 2021.

I did generate this diagram with an AI image tool, to be fully transparent — but every process, every label, every physical relationship in it came from my own understanding of the retrieval physics. I directed exactly what it needed to illustrate. It didn't invent any of the science.

Okay, this is the actual raw data page — but let me not just show you the maps, let me show you how this data actually gets pulled, because there's a few decisions buried in here that I think matter.

I'm acquiring NO2 through the Copernicus Data Space Ecosystem's Sentinel Hub Statistical API. And that word "Statistical" is doing a lot of work — it's a deliberate architectural choice. The alternative would've been downloading full-resolution Level-2 satellite swath files directly and computing the country averages myself, locally. For thirty countries across six years at monthly resolution, that's an enormous amount of raw data to pull and process. The Statistical API instead lets you hand Copernicus's own servers a country polygon and a time range, and it does the zonal aggregation server-side, and just hands you back the number. Same underlying satellite data, dramatically less data movement.

Inside the request, there's a small script called an evalscript that defines exactly which bands I want — the NO2 band itself, and a dataMask band, which flags which pixels are actually valid observations versus no-data. And I'm requesting the "sentinel-5p-l2" product specifically — Level 2, not Level 1. That distinction matters a lot, and it actually connects directly back to the physics I just showed you. Level 1 is calibrated radiance — the raw instrument signal converted into physical units, but not yet inverted into a gas concentration. Level 2 is the fully retrieved geophysical product — meaning ESA's own processing chain has already run that Beer-Lambert inversion and the air-mass-factor correction I described a minute ago. I'm not redoing that retrieval myself, I'm consuming the already-validated scientific output of it.

There's also a processing parameter in there, minQa set to 75 — a minimum quality-assurance threshold. Individual satellite observations get a quality score based on things like cloud contamination and sun-glint, and anything below that threshold gets excluded before it's even aggregated into my monthly average. Skip that filter, and you'd be quietly averaging in a bunch of noisy, low-confidence retrievals alongside good ones.

And just as a point of comparison, since I get asked this sometimes — before TROPOMI, the main instrument doing this kind of NO2 retrieval was OMI, aboard NASA's Aura satellite. OMI's native pixel size was roughly thirteen by twenty-four kilometers. TROPOMI brought that down to about three-and-a-half by five-and-a-half. That's not a small improvement — that resolution jump is genuinely what makes country-level, and in principle even city-level, analysis like this feasible at all.

NDVI, vegetation health, comes from Copernicus Global Land Service instead — this is the secondary outcome variable in this study, and it turns out to matter more than I originally expected. I'll come back to that later.

Quick side note on data quality — acquiring six years of this at country level wasn't one clean download. I hit a data-gap bug where December observations were silently missing for both NO2 and NDVI, some kind of seasonal edge case in how the API was chunking monthly requests near year-boundaries. The pipeline didn't error out, it just quietly returned less than it should have — took a bit of digging through row counts per month to even notice something was missing.

A difference-in-differences model isn't just "before versus after" — if I don't control for other things that could independently drive pollution, I'd be fooling myself with the result.

Let's start with GDP, since this one has a data-sourcing story worth telling. For the EU-27, GDP comes from Eurostat — the EU's own statistical office, methodologically consistent across all twenty-seven member states, which is exactly what you want for a panel regression. But Eurostat, obviously, doesn't publish figures for the UK, Norway, or Switzerland at the same level of integration I need here. So the three control countries come from a completely different source, the World Bank's Open Data API instead. And that creates a real metadata problem, not just a data-availability one — Eurostat reports in euros, World Bank reports in US dollars. So there's a documented, approximate EUR-to-USD currency conversion applied to harmonize the two sources before they can sit in the same column of the same dataset. It's approximate, and I say so directly in the documentation, because exchange rates fluctuate and I'm using a representative rate rather than month-by-month conversion — a small, honest limitation, but the kind of thing that's very easy to accidentally hide if you're not careful about documenting your own data pipeline.

GDP itself is log-scaled in this map, by the way, because it's heavily right-skewed — a handful of huge economies would otherwise wash out any visible variation among the rest. And using GDP as a control here is pretty standard practice across policy-evaluation economics generally — you'll see it in almost every DiD paper touching an environmental or economic outcome, because emissions and economic activity are so tightly coupled that without controlling for it, you genuinely can't tell if you're measuring a policy effect or just economic growth.

Land cover and elevation are static variables, so instead of entering them directly as regressors, they get fully absorbed by the country fixed effects in the actual model — still worth mapping here for context, just not part of the regression math itself.

Climate is the more interesting one, and let me actually show you the code for this, because the boundary-handling logic connects directly back to that QGIS view I showed you earlier.

This is the script that computes country-level temperature and precipitation from ERA5. Quick word on why ERA5 specifically — it's ECMWF's fifth-generation atmospheric reanalysis, which blends actual weather-station and satellite observations with a physical model to produce a complete, gap-free global grid, at roughly thirty-one kilometers and hourly resolution. The alternative would've been raw ground-station data, but station networks are sparse and unevenly distributed — Norway alone has huge areas with basically no dense station coverage — so you'd end up with wildly inconsistent data quality across thirty different countries. A reanalysis product gives you the same methodology, same resolution, same confidence everywhere, which is exactly what a panel regression needs.

In the code here, there's a function that loads all thirty country geometries — and you can see the same NUTS-plus-GADM logic from the QGIS boundaries I showed you earlier, implemented directly: it reads the NUTS Level 0 file, explicitly skips Norway and Switzerland from that file even though they're technically present in it, and then loads those two plus the UK separately from the three GADM files instead, appending them into one unified list so the rest of the pipeline can treat all thirty countries identically.

Then, for the actual zonal statistics — this is a proper geospatial technique, not just calling a library function and trusting it. I compute an affine transform directly from the ERA5 grid's latitude and longitude arrays, which defines exactly how pixel coordinates map to real-world geographic coordinates. Then, for every country polygon, I rasterize that polygon into a boolean mask on that same grid using geometry_mask, and only average the temperature and precipitation pixels that fall inside the mask. That's genuinely how raster-to-vector zonal statistics work under the hood — rasterize the vector boundary onto the raster's own grid, then mask and aggregate.

Small bug story here too, since I promised I'd be honest about these — merging the separate temperature and precipitation source files, I hit a duplicate-timestamp issue, caused by a conflicting internal metadata marker between the two files. If I hadn't caught that with a record-count check, it would've quietly biased the whole climate control variable with duplicated rows.

Headline number — DiD coefficient, negative one point four times ten to the minus six. Practically tiny. P-value 0.663 — not significant. 95% confidence interval spans zero completely.

In plain English — once I genuinely compared the EU against a real non-EU control group, the NO2 decline inside the EU is not statistically distinguishable from the decline in the UK, Norway, and Switzerland over the same period.

I want to be careful about how I phrase that, because "not significant" doesn't mean "proven to do nothing." At 80% power, this design can reliably detect an effect of roughly 28% of baseline EU NO2 or larger. So what this rules out is an effect of that size — it cannot rule out something smaller. That distinction matters a lot in causal work, and I think it's genuinely dishonest when studies gloss over it.

But this was not my first result. Let me actually open the code and show you exactly how this evolved, line by line, because I think that's more convincing than just telling you.

This first file is causal_inference dot py — my very first model, before the control group existed at all. Up top, just pandas, numpy, and statsmodels.

Lines 8 through 20 are the setup — I read the CSV, build a proper datetime column by concatenating year and zero-padded month, because monthly panel data needs a genuine time index, not two separate integer columns floating around independently. Then the treatment date, 30 June 2021, and a binary treatment flag — one if the observation falls after that date. And down at line 15 to 18, there's something worth pointing at specifically — an explicit linear time trend column, days since the dataset's start date. I added this because I already suspected the core problem this whole project eventually had to solve — that a simple before-after comparison risks the treatment coefficient just absorbing an ongoing, unrelated multi-year decline instead of anything policy-specific. So I tried to control for that trend directly, mathematically, inside this first model.

Lines 23 through 40 build the actual design matrix. Line 24 lists the controls — temperature, precipitation, GDP, and that time trend I just mentioned. Then — and this is a real bug I hit, worth explaining properly — building the country and month dummy variables with pandas' get_dummies function. In newer pandas versions, get_dummies returns boolean-typed columns by default now, not integers. And when you concatenate boolean columns into a numeric design matrix for an OLS fit, statsmodels' underlying linear algebra can behave inconsistently depending on the exact numpy version installed. So every single dummy variable in this project gets explicitly cast to float right after creation — you can see that dot astype float sitting right there on both dummy lines. Small line, but it's the difference between a fit that silently misbehaves and one that doesn't.

Line 45 onward is the actual regression — ordinary least squares, but fit with cluster-robust standard errors, clustered by NUTS ID, the EU country identifier. And this first model, run this way — it did come back significant. I remember being genuinely pleased with myself for about a day.

But here's my advice, honestly, whenever you get a clean significant result on your very first pass at anything — that's exactly the moment to be most suspicious, not most confident. So instead of trusting it, I ran a placebo test.

This second file, causal_inference_placebo dot py, is structurally almost identical — same imports, same control variables, same clustering approach. The only meaningful difference is one single line, where the real treatment date gets swapped out for a fake one, a full year earlier, at a point where nothing policy-relevant actually happened.

The logic here is simple but powerful — if a model with a completely fake treatment date still comes back statistically significant, that tells you the model isn't detecting a policy effect at all. It's just picking up whatever general trend already existed in the data, regardless of when you draw the line.

And the first time I ran this — it did come back significant. Almost as significant as the real result. That's the exact moment you realize your original finding wasn't measuring the policy. Not a great feeling in the moment, honestly, but genuinely the single most useful five minutes of the entire project.

That failure is exactly what forced me to go build a real control group — which is that QGIS boundary work I showed you earlier, on the Study Design page. Two failed models led directly to that decision.

Which brings me to the third file — causal_inference_final_did dot py — the one that actually produces the honest headline result.

First thing worth noticing, right at the top — the data path here is different. It's reading master_dataset_control dot csv, not master_dataset dot csv like the first two files. That's not a typo, that's deliberate — this is the expanded thirty-country dataset, built specifically after the control group existed.

Lines 8 through 19 — instead of a simple treatment flag, there's now a "post" flag, meaning after the treatment date, regardless of country. And then, right at line 16, the actual core of the whole project — did_interaction equals treatment_group times post. That's the entire difference-in-differences estimator, in one line. It only equals one when both conditions are true simultaneously — an EU country, and after the policy date. Every other combination — control country before, control country after, EU country before — is zero. That interaction term's coefficient is the exact number I showed you a minute ago.

Down in the regression itself, lines 30 through 34 have a comment I want to actually read out, because it's a genuine econometric convention, not just a code comment — treatment_group and post are included as their own separate regressors, as main effects, alongside the interaction term. That's standard difference-in-differences practice — if you only included the interaction term and left out the main effects, you'd be conflating a simple level difference between the EU and control group with the actual policy-caused change, which would completely invalidate the causal interpretation.

Also — notice the clustering variable changed too. The first model clustered by NUTS ID. This one clusters by a column just called "country," because once you're mixing EU countries, which are NUTS-coded, with UK, Norway, and Switzerland, which aren't NUTS countries at all, you need one unified country-coding scheme across the whole panel — which is exactly the same boundary-harmonization problem I described with the QGIS layers earlier, just showing up again here in the regression's own metadata.

And that clustering approach itself — clustering standard errors by country in panel data like this — comes from a paper that's basically required reading in this space, Bertrand, Duflo, and Mullainathan, from 2004. It's the paper that convinced a whole generation of applied economists that skipping this step in panel data is a very easy way to fool yourself into false significance, because repeated monthly observations from the same country are obviously correlated with each other over time, and ordinary OLS standard errors don't know that.

I also stress-tested this further with an event-study — estimating the effect separately for every single quarter from 2019 to 2024 instead of one average. Twenty of twenty-three quarters show no significant effect, both before and after treatment, which supports the model's core parallel-trends assumption.

Here's the full regression table if you want the actual numbers, and this bar chart is really the visual version of the same finding — EU and control group, both declining at a pretty similar rate.

One more result I want to flag, because leaving it out would be a little dishonest. Same design, run on NDVI as a secondary outcome — and unlike NO2, this one did come back significant. A relative decline in EU vegetation health versus the control group, p equals 0.012.

I want to be precise about what I am and am not claiming here — I am not saying the Climate Law damaged vegetation. It's an emissions-focused policy, not a land-use one, and this doesn't control for land-use change, drought, or agricultural-policy differences. It's an honest, robust pattern that deserves further investigation, not a causal claim I'm confident enough to make yet.

Last thing on this — my control group is only three countries. That's a real constraint on power, and I say so directly: this design can rule out an effect of 28% or larger, but genuinely cannot rule out something smaller. I also deliberately scoped out an entire module — an economic-efficiency ranking of the different policies — because ranking by cost-per-unit-improvement only makes sense if you've actually found a significant improvement to rank against.
